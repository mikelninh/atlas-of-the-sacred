# Göbekli Tepe evidence map

**Status:** working evidence architecture for the second deep portal  
**Editorial owner:** Atlas editorial  
**Research cut:** 2026-07-29  
**Review requirement:** early-Neolithic archaeology specialist before V1 final

## Portal thesis

Göbekli Tepe is not presented as a miraculous temple that single-handedly created civilisation. It is presented as a long-lived early-Neolithic place where people built, inhabited, modified and gathered around exceptional architecture while lifeways across Upper Mesopotamia were changing.

## Central question

> Did settled society make monumental gathering possible—or did gathering around shared worlds help new forms of society emerge?

The Atlas does not answer this causally. It lets visitors inspect the material conditions and competing interpretations.

## Vocabulary guardrails

Prefer:

- monumental communal or special-purpose buildings;
- T-shaped limestone pillars;
- anthropomorphic features;
- Pre-Pottery Neolithic communities;
- apparent domestic or non-monumental structures;
- regional social network;
- changing building biographies;
- unresolved purpose and meaning.

Avoid as unqualified fact:

- “the world’s first temple”;
- “religion invented civilisation”;
- “purely ritual centre”;
- “deliberately buried all at once”;
- “observatory” or “zodiac”; 
- named gods, priests or mythic beings;
- exact workforce or transport sequence.

## Cluster 1 — Time and occupation

### Research question

How long was Göbekli Tepe used, and what changed across that history?

### Established observations

- Monumental round-oval and rectangular structures date broadly between 9600 and 8200 BCE in the UNESCO synthesis.
- The project distinguishes earlier monumental circular buildings with large T-pillars from later, generally smaller rectangular architecture.
- The mound preserves repeated construction, modification and deposition rather than one frozen building event.

### Interpretive boundary

Chronological phases do not map cleanly onto a simple progression from “ritual” to “domestic,” mobile to settled, or primitive to advanced.

### Public misconception to prevent

“Göbekli Tepe appeared suddenly, operated briefly as one temple complex, and was buried in a single event.”

### Interaction opportunity

A time slider changes the enclosure field from large circular buildings to denser rectangular architecture, while uncertain overlaps remain visible.

## Cluster 2 — A regional Neolithic landscape

### Research question

Was Göbekli Tepe an isolated anomaly?

### Established observations

- Related T-shaped pillars and imagery occur at contemporaneous sites across Upper Mesopotamia.
- Current Taş Tepeler research situates Göbekli Tepe among multiple early-Neolithic sites around Şanlıurfa.
- UNESCO interprets shared architectural and visual forms as evidence of close regional social networks.

### Interpretive boundary

Shared forms do not prove one political state, single religion, ethnic identity or centrally controlled building programme.

### Interaction opportunity

The visitor begins with multiple hills appearing across a regional horizon before the view moves toward Göbekli Tepe.

## Cluster 3 — Monumental and apparent domestic architecture

### Research question

What kinds of buildings stood at the site?

### Established observations

- Monumental communal or special-purpose buildings include paired central pillars and surrounding pillars integrated with walls and benches.
- Smaller, less architecturally complex structures have been interpreted as apparent domestic buildings.
- Project material reports evidence associated with habitation and daily activity, including fire installations, food remains, water collection and dense building remains.

### Interpretive boundary

“Domestic” and “ritual” are modern analytical categories. A building can support dwelling, gathering, memory, work and ceremony without fitting only one label.

### Public misconception to prevent

“No one lived at Göbekli Tepe.”

## Cluster 4 — T-shaped pillars as embodied forms

### Research question

What can be said about the pillars as representations?

### Established observations

- Several pillars carry arms, hands, belts and loincloths.
- These features support describing at least some T-pillars as abstract anthropomorphic forms.
- Central pillars can exceed five metres; the pillars also served architectural functions.

### Interpretive boundary

Anthropomorphic does not identify the figures as gods, ancestors, priests, rulers or named beings.

### Interaction opportunity

A Body mode reveals only documented features. Interpretation remains in a separate layer.

---

## Cluster 5 — Animal imagery and relations

### Research question

What animals are depicted, and how were images organised?

### Established observations

- Wild animals dominate the relief repertoire, including birds, predators, foxes, snakes, boar and smaller species.
- Motif frequency varies between excavated buildings.
- Animal images share pillars with anthropomorphic features and abstract signs.

### Interpretive boundary

Species identification and placement are observations. Totems, clans, constellations, myths and catastrophe records remain interpretations unless independent evidence supports them.

### Interaction opportunity

A Relation mode lets visitors select an animal image, see its documented placement and compare several scholarly readings without selecting a winner.

## Cluster 6 — Stone, quarry and communal work

### Research question

What material and labour conditions can be reconstructed?

### Established observations

- Pillars were carved from local limestone on the surrounding plateau.
- Quarry traces and unfinished workpieces preserve evidence of extraction.
- The central pillars of Building D are about 5.5 metres high and estimated around 8–10 metric tonnes in project communication.

### Interpretive boundary

Local quarry evidence does not preserve the complete workforce size, social authority, transport rig or raising sequence.

### Interaction opportunity

Visitors trace a pillar from limestone bank to unfinished workpiece to erected form, with every unknown step visibly interrupted.

---

## Cluster 7 — Food, cereals and gathering

### Research question

How were work and gathering provisioned?

### Established observations

- More than 7,000 grinding tools were analysed in a PLOS ONE study.
- Use-wear indicates substantial cereal processing.
- Grinding tools occur in rectangular buildings, open spaces and deposits associated with monumental buildings.
- Animal remains and food-processing evidence show that eating and provisioning were important parts of site life.

### Interpretive boundary

Cereal processing does not by itself prove domesticated crops, one central feast, beer production at every event or that feasting caused monumentality.

### Interaction opportunity

A provisioning layer reveals the repeated labour behind gathering: grain, grinding, water, tools and movement rather than a single spectacular banquet.

---

## Cluster 8 — Building biographies and filling

### Research question

How did buildings change, close and become buried?

### Established observations

- Buildings were repaired, modified and filled through complex depositional histories.
- Some deposits support intentional placement or backfilling.
- Sloped rubble, collapse and sediment movement also contributed to sealing buildings.
- Architectural sequence remains under active study.

### Interpretive boundary

The whole site should not be described as deliberately and ceremonially buried all at once.

### Interaction opportunity

Layers accumulate through several selectable processes: repair, intentional deposition, later building, collapse and slope movement.

---

## Cluster 9 — Social organisation

### Research question

What social capacities did monumental construction require?

### Established observations

- Quarrying, carving, transporting, feeding and maintaining large buildings required coordination and specialised knowledge.
- Architectural planning research proposes deliberate geometric relationships among several monumental buildings.

### Open interpretations

- seasonal cooperation;
- household or lineage groups;
- specialised craftspeople;
- temporary leadership;
- ritual authority;
- emerging hierarchy.

### Interpretive boundary

Coordination does not automatically prove kings, coercive labour, a permanent priesthood or a fully stratified society.

---

## Cluster 10 — Purpose and meaning

### Research question

What were the buildings for?

### Established observations

- Architecture, imagery, food activity, deposition and repeated gathering all mattered.
- UNESCO describes likely social events and rituals; older scholarship often emphasised sanctuary or cult use.
- Current evidence also supports habitation and daily activity at the site.

### Open interpretations

- communal assembly;
- ritual performance;
- funerary association;
- memory and identity;
- feasting;
- work-group or lineage buildings;
- combinations that changed over time.

### Interpretive boundary

No surviving text supplies the builders’ explanation. The Atlas must not turn a compelling interpretation into recovered belief.

---

## Cluster 11 — Conservation and stewardship

### Research question

What does responsible discovery require now?

### Established observations

- Excavation destroys context even as it produces knowledge.
- The DAI project describes intentionally limiting excavation to what is necessary for unresolved research questions.
- UNESCO’s 2025 decision urged limiting further excavation and intervention pending a comprehensive conservation and revised management plan, except for urgent stabilisation and preventive maintenance.

### Interpretive boundary

More excavation is not automatically more knowledge. Preservation, documentation and local stewardship are part of the evidence system.

### Interaction opportunity

The portal ends by pulling back from the excavated enclosure to the mostly unexcavated mound and asking what should remain undisturbed.
